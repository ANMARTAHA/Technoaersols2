Overview

The core rules and facts — no lecture, just what you need to lock in.

01

Valve purpose

Expel the contents in the desired form, at the desired rate, and — for metered valves — in the correct dose.

02

Material rule

All valve materials must be inert to the formulation and FDA-approved. Common materials: plastic, rubber, aluminum, stainless steel.

03

Orifice rule

Larger orifices + less propellant → foams & solid streams. Smaller orifices + more propellant → sprays & mists.

04

MDI metering

A dual-valve mechanism alternates the metering chamber between "fill from container" and "release to atmosphere."

05

Cold filling

Concentrate + propellant cooled to -34.5°C to -40°C. Cannot be used for aqueous systems — water freezes.

06

Pressure filling

Used for most pharmaceutical aerosols. Two advantages: less moisture contamination, less propellant lost.

07

Discharge rate

Grams discharged per unit time, found by weighing a filled aerosol before and after a timed discharge.

08

Storage rule

Store between 15°C and 30°C. Cold canisters give a weaker-than-normal spray.

09

Label warnings

Never puncture, never store near heat/flame, never incinerate.

10

Spacers

Placed between mouthpiece and mouth; separate actuation from inhalation by 3–5 seconds; cut oropharyngeal deposition.

11

DPI vs pMDI

DPIs are propellant-free and breath-actuated, but depend on the patient's own inhalation strength and are humidity-sensitive.

12

Foam phase rule

Propellant in the internal phase → stable foam. Propellant in the external phase → spray or quick-breaking foam.

Advantages of DPIs over pMDIs

  • Propellant-free; usually no excipient besides a lactose carrier.
  • Breath-actuated — no need to coordinate actuation with inhalation.
  • Can deliver larger drug doses than pressurized MDIs.

Result: Stable Foam

  • Propellant sits in the internal (discontinuous) phase.
  • The product discharges as a stable foam that holds its structure.
  • Surfactants keep the gas dispersed within the liquid phase.
